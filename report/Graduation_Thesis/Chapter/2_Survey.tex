\section{Status survey}
\label{section:2.1}

A thorough survey of the current landscape is essential to identify the precise software requirements and ensure the proposed solution effectively addresses practical challenges. This survey analyzes data from three primary sources: target users in flood-prone areas, existing emergency response systems, and similar location-sharing applications on the market.

\subsection{Analysis of Existing Systems and Similar Applications}
\label{subsection:2.1.1}

Currently, several applications are utilized by the community during emergencies; however, they exhibit fundamental architectural and functional gaps when applied to severe flood scenarios and post-disaster relief.

\textbf{Zalo / Instant Messaging Applications:} Zalo is the most popular communication tool in Vietnam. Users can share their live location with contacts; however, this feature is strictly session-based and requires manual activation within a specific chat box. Crucially, it lacks continuous background tracking and entirely fails when the Internet connection drops. It also does not provide a centralized mechanism to broadcast SOS signals directly to authorized rescue teams.

\textbf{Web-based SOS Platforms (An toàn cứu hộ):} These are dedicated platforms designed to transmit distress signals. While directly connected to rescue operations, their primary limitation is their reliance on web browsers, meaning they operate strictly online. If a victim loses network connectivity, the system immediately drops the connection, preventing any signal transmission. Furthermore, these platforms often lack features for victims to share their status with family members.

\textbf{"Whoo" and Social Location Apps:} "Whoo" is a mobile application designed for sharing locations among friends. It successfully implements a mechanism to save the user's last known location (last-location caching). However, it is primarily built for social networking rather than emergency response, meaning it cannot link victims with professional rescue units. Additionally, the application has been reported to suffer from synchronization instability.

None of the aforementioned systems incorporate functionalities to manage post-disaster charity campaigns, leaving the transparency of relief distribution entirely unaddressed.

\subsection{Essential software features to be developed}
\label{subsection:2.1.2}

Derived from the critical analysis of existing solutions and the specific demands of flood rescue operations, the proposed FloodHelper system must integrate a comprehensive suite of functionalities. These essential features are categorized into four primary subsystems:

\subsubsection{Rescue and Location Tracking Subsystem (Mobile Application)}
\label{subsubsection:a.}

\textbf{Background Synchronization and Privacy Control:} The application must maintain a persistent background isolate to periodically capture and push GPS coordinates. Crucially, the user's real-time online/offline status is continuously embedded directly within this location data payload. Users can share this comprehensive real-time data with connected friends. Additionally, a "freeze" mechanism is implemented, allowing users to pause live tracking for specific friends, who will then only have access to the user's last known location and status.

\textbf{MQTT-Driven SOS Broadcasting:} Victims can trigger distress signals encompassing their current coordinates and specific physical conditions. To guarantee low latency and high transmission success rates in intermittent networks, all location sharing and SOS broadcasting strictly utilize the MQTT protocol.

\textbf{Rescue Coordination and Visualization:} Rescuers can retrieve a dynamically updated list of active SOS signals, automatically sorted by severity to prioritize urgent cases. During an active rescue, victims can view the rescuer's live location on their screen. Once a victim is successfully rescued, the system immediately halts the specific SOS broadcast, notifying all other rescuers to prevent redundant deployment.

\textbf{Inter-Rescuer Collaboration:} Rescuers operating within the same administrative ward can share live locations with each other to orchestrate joint operations. Furthermore, a dedicated distress feature allows rescuers to broadcast their own SOS signals to peer rescuers in case of secondary emergencies.

\textbf{Offline Tracking and Perimeter Establishment:} In scenarios involving complete network disconnection, Rescuers can securely query the MQTT broker to retrieve a victim's last recorded coordinates along with their last known connection status. This allows rescue teams to establish a reliable search perimeter even when the victim is entirely offline.

\subsubsection{Transparent Charity Management Subsystem (Mobile Application)}
\label{subsubsection:b.}

\textbf{Campaign Lifecycle and Spatial Tracking:} The system manages the strict state transitions of a charity campaign. During the distribution phase, the system visually marks the Benefactor's real-time geographical positions on the map, providing spatial proof of relief delivery.

\textbf{Donation Processing and Traceability:} The application integrates the VietQR API to automatically generate payment QR codes for donors. Users can seamlessly donate and track the detailed transaction flow, ensuring financial transparency.

\textbf{Campaign Announcements:} Benefactors are equipped with tools to publish official announcements, keeping donors updated on the campaign's progress and relief distribution.

\subsubsection{Administration and Moderation Subsystem (Web Platform)}
\label{subsubsection:c.}

\textbf{Role Verification and Campaign Moderation:} A dedicated web dashboard empowers Authorities to rigorously verify the credentials of registered Rescuers and Benefactors. Authorities govern campaign lifecycles by approving activations, monitoring ongoing states, and executing immediate suspensions if fraudulent activities are detected.

\textbf{Regional Broadcast Notifications:} Authorities have the capability to publish official warnings, weather updates, or emergency directives to all users residing within their specific administrative jurisdictions.

\textbf{System Administration:} A high-level Admin role is established to manage the overall user base, specifically focusing on creating, assigning, and updating Authority accounts.

\subsubsection{Social Connectivity Subsystem (Mobile Application)}
\label{subsubsection:d.}

\textbf{Network Establishment:} To facilitate the foundational location-sharing feature among peers, the application allows users to establish connections and add friends via email addresses securely.

\subsection{General use case diagram}
\label{subsection:2.2.1}

The general use case diagram of the FloodHelper system, as illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:Fig221}, delineates the primary interactions between the participating actors and the system's core functionalities. The architecture incorporates five distinct actors, each assigned specific roles and operational permissions. The Normal User acts as the base actor, representing any registered individual within the system, typically residents in flood-prone areas seeking connection and location sharing. Two specialized actors, the Rescuer and the Benefactor, structurally inherit all properties and use cases from the Normal User. The Rescuer represents authorized personnel or volunteers actively conducting emergency rescue operations, whereas the Benefactor denotes individuals or organizations initiating and managing charity campaigns. On the management side, the Authority acts as a verified official responsible for overseeing regional operations, validating user credentials, and ensuring charity transparency within a specific jurisdiction. Finally, the Admin serves as the overarching system administrator tasked with centralized user management.

The system encompasses several main use cases to fulfill its dual objectives of rescue coordination and relief management. Core security and maintenance operations, specifically authenticate and manage my account, are universally accessible to all actors. For general engagement, the Normal User is authorized to interact with map, make friend, and view public announcements, establishing a foundational network for location tracking. Crucially, the Normal User is also directly linked to the manage distress signal use case, empowering victims to actively broadcast SOS alerts and share their critical conditions during emergencies. Concurrently, the Rescuer interacts with this same use case to receive, track, and resolve these broadcasted emergency alerts. The manage charity campaign use case is collaboratively operated; Benefactors utilize it to initiate and update relief funds, while Authorities interact with the same use case to monitor, approve, or suspend these campaigns. Furthermore, Authorities manage regional communications through the CRD public announcements use case and ensure operational integrity by executing the review role requests and review profile-updating requests use cases. Lastly, the Admin solely performs the manage user use case to control high-level account assignments and system configurations.

\subsection{Business process}
\label{subsection:2.2.10}

This section outlines the core operational workflows of the application, illustrating the interactions between system actors (Benefactors, Rescuers, Users, and Authorities) and the platform. The business processes are designed to ensure transparency in charity campaigns, accuracy in financial tracking, and efficiency in emergency rescue coordination. The following subsections detail the logic and sequential steps for the primary modules: Update Personal Information, Broadcast signal and Rescue victim, Create and Manage Charity Campaign.

\subsubsection{Update Personal Information}

The business process for updating personal information, as illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:Fig2210a}, outlines the structured workflow for users to modify their data or request a role escalation. Upon viewing their profile, users can either initiate a role request if their profile is already complete, or proceed to edit their personal information. When editing, standard users can apply updates to their profiles directly. However, if the user holds a specialized role, such as a Rescuer or Benefactor, the system mandates the creation of a formal profile-updating request rather than applying the changes instantaneously.

Once a profile-updating request or a role request is generated, it enters a pending state. During this phase, the initiating user retains the autonomy to revoke their request at any time, which immediately terminates the process. If the request remains active, it is forwarded to the Authority for official moderation. The Authority evaluates the submitted credentials and makes a definitive decision to either approve or reject the profile modifications or role escalations based on the reliability of the provided information. This authoritative action ultimately resolves the request and concludes the business process.

\subsubsection{Create and Manage Charity Campaign}

The business process for creating and managing a charity campaign, as depicted in Figure \ref{fig:Fig2210c}, orchestrates a comprehensive, multi-actor workflow involving the Benefactor, Authority, the FloodHelper system, and the Normal User. \subsection{Description of use case "Broadcast signal"}
\label{section:2.3.1}

\subsubsection{Use Case Name}
UC001-BS

\subsubsection{Pre-condition}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Normal User, Rescuer and Friend do not lose Internet completely. Besides, they are logged into the application.
    \item Background service in user app operates normally.
    \item MQTT Client subscribed topics "current-location" and "signal" before.
    \item Rescuer subscribed topic "rescuer/common/<ward\_id>", "rescuer-location/<ward\_id>" before.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Main Flow}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item User fills in the signal information and clicks "broadcast signal".
    \item User Frontend subscribes to the topic current-ward/<user\_id>.
    \item User Frontend triggers two parallel processes:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Sends location (with isSos == true) to the topic current-location.
        \item Sends a signal creation message to the topic "signal".
    \end{itemize}

    \vspace{1em}
    \item[] \textbf{Parallel Process A:} Signal Creation Handling
    \item MQTT Broker forwards the signal message to the MQTT Client.
    \item MQTT Client receives the message and attempts to get ward\_id in the cache based on user\_id.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Sub-step: If ward\_id does not exist in the cache or is expired, it gets the ward based on the location and caches ward\_id with key = user\_id.
    \end{itemize}
    \item MQTT Client sends the signal data and ward\_id to the Backend.
    \item Backend receives the signal creation request and checks for the existence of the user's broadcasting signal.
    \item Assuming no broadcasting signal ([== null]), Backend:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Saves data to the database.
        \item Gets the relevant rescuer list.
        \item Sends push notifications to rescuers.
        \item Creates daily announcements for rescuers.
        \item Sends a response: "create successfully" and data back to the User Frontend.
    \end{itemize}

    \vspace{1em}
    \item[] \textbf{Parallel Process B:} Location Broadcast Handling
    \item MQTT Broker forwards the location message to the MQTT Client.
    \item MQTT Client receives the message and performs two parallel tasks:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Task 1 (To Friends): Publishes the location to the topic "<user\_id>/<friend\_id>/last-location". MQTT Broker forwards the message to the Friend Frontend.
        \item Task 2 (To Rescuers/System): Gets ward\_id in the cache based on user\_id (handling cache miss/expiration similarly to step 5).
    \end{itemize}
    \item MQTT Client checks if the ward has changed. If [old ward != new ward], it publishes the ward ID to the topic "current-ward/<user\_id>".
    \begin{itemize}
        \item MQTT Broker forwards it to the User Frontend.
        \item User Frontend receives ward data and subscribes to the topic rescuer-location/<new\_ward\_id> (if not subscribed yet).
    \end{itemize}
    \item MQTT Client checks the user's role. Since [user is not rescuer], it publishes the location to the topic "rescuer/common/<ward\_id>".
    \item MQTT Broker forwards this location to rescuers.
    \item Rescuer Frontend receives the location data, unifies pins, and displays them on the map.
    \item All parallel processes converge, and the activity terminates.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Alternative / Exception Flows}
\textbf{Alternative Flow 1: When User is a Rescuer} \par
This flow is identical to the Main Flow up to Step 11. The divergence happens at Step 12 when checking the role.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item MQTT Client checks the user's role. Since [user is rescuer], it publishes the location to the topic "rescuer-location/<ward\_id>".
    \item MQTT Broker forwards the location to other rescuers and the victim.
    \item Rescuer Frontend receives the location data, unifies pins, and displays them on the map.
    \item User Frontend (acting as the victim tracking the rescuer) receives location data from the rescuer, unifies pins, and displays them on the map.
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{0.5em}
\textbf{Alternative Flow 2: Signal Already Exists (Broadcasting Signal != null)} \par
This exception occurs in the Backend during the Signal Creation Handling (Step 7 of Main Flow).
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Backend checks the existence of the user's broadcasting signal.
    \item The condition [!= null] is met (meaning the user already has an active signal).
    \item Backend skips saving data and notifying rescuers.
    \item Backend immediately sends the response: "Stop/Update signal, please".
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Post-condition}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item On Success (Normal User): A new emergency signal is saved in the database. Notifications and announcements are sent to relevant rescuers. The user's location is successfully broadcasted and displayed on the maps of their friends and local rescuers. The User Frontend is subscribed to the appropriate ward topics.
    \item On Success (Rescuer): Similar to the Normal User, but the rescuer's live location is specifically broadcasted to and displayed on the maps of other rescuers and the victims.
    \item On Failure/Exception (Signal Already Exists): No new signal is created in the database. No new notifications are sent to rescuers. The user is prompted to stop or update their current active signal.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Description of use case "Handle signal"}
\label{section:2.3.2}

\subsubsection{Use Case Name}
UC002-HS

\subsubsection{Pre-condition}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The Rescuer is logged into the application and is viewing the map with active emergency pins.
    \item The Victim (User) has an active broadcasting signal.
    \item Required topic subscriptions:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Victim Frontend must be currently subscribed to "<user\_id>/rescuer-reply" to listen for incoming rescue confirmations.
        \item Rescuer Frontends (both the acting Rescuer and Other Rescuers) must be subscribed to "rescuer/common/<ward\_id>" to listen for global state changes of emergency signals in their area.
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Main Flow}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Rescuer clicks a user's emergency pin on the map.
    \item Rescuer Frontend displays a bubble speech containing the signal's details.
    \item Rescuer clicks the "handle" button within the bubble speech.
    \item Rescuer Frontend publishes a signal handling message to the MQTT topic "rescuer/handle/<ward\_id>".
    \item MQTT Broker forwards this message to the MQTT Client.
    \item MQTT Client receives the message and sends a request to the Backend to handle the signal.
    \item Backend processes the request by executing the following actions sequentially:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Updates the signal state in the database
        \item Sends a push notification to the Victim.
        \item Creates a daily announcement for the Victim.
        \item Returns a success response back to the MQTT Client.
    \end{itemize}
    \item MQTT Client receives the response and triggers two parallel broadcasting processes:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Parallel Process A (Notify Victim):
        \begin{enumerate}
            \item Publishes a handling message to the topic "<user\_id>/rescuer-reply".
            \item MQTT Broker forwards this message to the Victim Frontend.
            \item Victim Frontend receives the handling message, unsubscribes from the topics "rescuer-location/<ward\_id>" and "current-ward/<user\_id>", set isSos = false in each message published to MQTT Broker, and updates the UI (remove rescuer pin, update button state).
        \end{enumerate}
        \item Parallel Process B (Notify Rescuers):
        \begin{enumerate}
            \item Publishes a handling message to the topic "rescuer/common/<ward\_id>".
            \item MQTT Broker forwards this message to the rescuers in that ward.
            \item Both the acting Rescuer Frontend and the Other Rescuer Frontend receive the handling message and update their respective UIs
        \end{enumerate}
    \end{itemize}
    \item The activity converges and terminates after all respective frontends have successfully updated their UIs.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Alternative / Exception Flows}
\textbf{Alternative Flow 1: When Victim is a Rescuer} \par
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The flow executes identically to the Main Flow from Step 1 to Step 8.
    \item In Parallel Process A, the Victim Frontend receives the handling message from "<user\_id>/rescuer-reply".
    \item Because the Victim is also an active Rescuer, they must maintain operational awareness of the area. Therefore, the Victim Frontend skips unsubscribing from "rescuer-location/<ward\_id>". It only unsubscribes from "current-ward/<user\_id>" and updates the UI to reflect that their personal distress signal is being handled.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Post-condition}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Database State: The specific emergency signal's state is successfully updated in the database to reflect that it has been acknowledged and is being handled.
    \item Victim State: The Victim is successfully informed via push notifications, daily announcements, and real-time UI updates that a rescuer is responding to their signal. The Victim's app (if a Normal User) disconnects from unnecessary location topics to save bandwidth and processing.
    \item System UI State: All rescuer applications in the designated ward are synchronized; their maps are updated to show that the specific distress signal is currently being handled, preventing duplicated rescue efforts.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Description of use case "Revoke signal"}
\label{section:2.3.3}

\subsubsection{Use Case Name}
UC003-RS

\subsubsection{Pre-condition}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The Victim is logged into the application and currently has an active broadcasting emergency signal.
    \item Required topic subscriptions:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Rescuer Frontends in the affected area must be actively subscribed to "rescuer/common/<ward\_id>" to listen for real-time updates regarding signal cancellations or status changes.
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Main Flow}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Victim clicks the "revoke signal" button on their interface.
    \item Victim Frontend publishes a signal revoking message to the MQTT topic "signal".
    \item The flow then splits into two parallel processes that execute concurrently:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Parallel Process A (Local and UI Update):
        \begin{enumerate}
            \item Victim Frontend updates the signal state on its local storage.
            \item Victim Frontend sets the flag isSos = false in each subsequent message published to the MQTT Broker (ensuring future location updates are no longer treated as emergency signals).
            \item Victim Frontend updates the UI to reflect that the signal has been successfully revoked.
        \end{enumerate}
        \item Parallel Process B (Backend and Rescuer Notification):
        \begin{enumerate}
            \item MQTT Broker forwards the signal revoking message to the MQTT Client.
            \item MQTT Client receives the message and sends a request to the Backend to update the signal state.
            \item Backend receives the request, updates the signal state in the database (marking it as canceled/revoked), and sends a response back.
            \item Upon receiving the response, the MQTT Client publishes a signal revoking message to the topic "rescuer/common/<ward\_id>".
            \item MQTT Broker forwards this message to the related rescuers in that ward.
            \item Rescuer Frontend receives the message and updates its UI
        \end{enumerate}
    \end{itemize}
    \item Both parallel processes converge, and the activity successfully terminates.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Alternative / Exception Flows}
\textbf{Exception 1: Network Failure / MQTT Broker Unreachable} \par
This exception occurs if the Victim's device loses internet connection right after clicking the button.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Victim Frontend successfully executes Parallel Process A (updates local storage, sets isSos = false, and updates local UI).
    \item The publish action to the topic "signal" fails to reach the MQTT Broker.
    \item Backend and Rescuer Frontends are not notified immediately.
    \item System behavior: The system must rely on MQTT Quality of Service (QoS) configurations to queue the message and retry publishing once the connection is restored, ensuring eventual consistency between the local state and the Backend.
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{0.5em}
\textbf{Exception 2: Backend Update Failure} \par
This exception occurs in Parallel Process B (Backend lane).
\begin{enumerate}
    \item MQTT Client requests the Backend to update the signal state.
    \item Backend encounters a database error or validation issue and fails to update the signal state.
    \item Backend returns an error response to the MQTT Client.
    \item MQTT Client aborts publishing the revocation message to the "rescuer/common/<ward\_id>" topic.
    \item The Rescuer Frontend continues to display the signal as active until the issue is resolved or manually synced.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Post-condition}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Database State: The user's emergency signal is marked as inactive/revoked in the backend system.
    \item Local State: The victim's local device recognizes the signal as revoked, and all future location broadcasts operate in normal mode (isSos = false).
    \item System UI State: All related rescuer applications in the designated ward have their maps updated, clearing the revoked distress signal to prevent unnecessary deployment.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Description of use case "Publish public announcement"}
\label{section:2.3.4}

\subsubsection{Use Case Name}
UC004-PAP

\subsubsection{Pre-condition}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The Authority user is authenticated and logged into the system (Authority Frontend).
    \item The Authority has the appropriate administrative privileges to create and broadcast announcements to their designated ward.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Main Flow}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Authority clicks the "Create Announcements" button.
    \item Authority Frontend displays the announcement creation form.
    \item Authority enters the title and caption for the announcement.
    \item Assuming the authority does not want to add a file, the Authority Frontend sends the form directly to the Backend.
    \item Backend receives the request.
    \item Backend inserts the new announcement into the database with the default state fileUrl = NULL.
    \item Backend checks if a file is attached to the request. Since there is no file, it skips the cloud upload process.
    \item Backend sends a push notification to users residing in the same ward.
    \item Backend sends a success response back to the Authority Frontend.
    \item Authority Frontend updates the UI to reflect the newly published announcement, and the activity terminates.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Alternative / Exception Flows}
\textbf{Exception 1: Database Insert Failure} \par
\begin{enumerate}
    \item If the Backend fails to insert the new announcement into the database (e.g., due to a database connection error or schema validation failure), the flow is interrupted. The Backend returns an error response, and the Authority Frontend displays an error message without updating the active announcements list.
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{0.5em}
\textbf{Exception 2: Cloud Upload Failure } \par
\begin{enumerate}
    \item If the condition [file is attached to request] is met but the Backend fails to upload the file to the cloud service, the system should ideally catch this exception, return an error response to the Authority Frontend, and halt the push notification process to avoid broadcasting an incomplete announcement.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Post-condition}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Database State: A new public announcement record is successfully created and persisted in the database.
    \item System/Cloud State: If an attachment was included, the file is successfully stored in the cloud storage provider.
    \item User State: Citizens/Users located in the authority's ward have received a push notification alerting them of the new announcement.
    \item UI State: The Authority's frontend dashboard is refreshed and displays the newly published announcement.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Non-functional requirement}
\label{section:2.4}
Performance (Latency): Real-time location tracking and signal broadcasting via the MQTT broker must maintain a delivery latency of under 500ms to ensure accurate, up-to-date mapping of victims and rescuers.

Reliability (Network Resilience): The mobile application must utilize delay-tolerant networking strategies and background isolates to queue emergency signals and location data locally, ensuring eventual transmission even when operating in unstable or heavily degraded network environments.

Scalability (Concurrency): The containerized backend infrastructure must be capable of automatically scaling to handle sudden, massive spikes in concurrent connections and API requests during widespread emergency events without system degradation.

Maintainability (Architecture): The system's codebase must strictly adhere to clean architecture principles (such as the MVVM pattern for the frontend) to ensure straightforward unit testing, predictable state management, and the seamless integration of future communication protocols.
